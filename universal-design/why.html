Why universal design isn’t optional

Access is table-stakes. The web is a public space; excluding people by vision, hearing, mobility, cognition, bandwidth or device undercuts the point of the web. Accessibility requirements are codified in WCAG, which many institutions adopt by policy or law. Building it in is cheaper than retrofits and reduces legal/support risk.

It measurably improves products. WCAG 2.2’s newer criteria (e.g., Focus Appearance and Target Size) were added because users literally couldn’t see focus or hit tiny taps. Fixing those raises completion rates on forms and navs, especially on mobile.

It accelerates delivery. Semantic HTML exposes name/role/value to Assistive Tech via accessibility APIs, meaning less custom JS/ARIA and fewer bugs. Authoring-practice-aligned patterns (e.g., disclosure, tabs) avoid inventing bespoke controls. :contentReference[oaicite:2]{index=2}

It future-proofs content. Native tech keeps getting better: MathML Core is shipping broadly, enabling accessible math without heavy client JS. When platforms improve, your semantic content improves with them.

Real scenarios you design for

  • Bright sun on a cracked phone: high contrast + readable focus rings mean the menu is still operable.
  • Low-bandwidth dorm Wi-Fi: captions & transcripts keep lecture videos useful and searchable.
  • Repetitive strain flare-up: full keyboard support and visible focus keep productivity up.
  • STEM homework with a screen reader: MathML Core enables direct reading & navigation.

Principle

Design for the edges and everyone benefits—fewer support tickets, faster UX, better SEO, better DX.

TL;DR

  • It’s ethical.
  • It reduces risk.
  • It improves core business metrics.
  • It makes better, faster products.
